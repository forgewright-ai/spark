# Chapter 4: The Water Cycle

## 4.1 Where the water is

Almost all of the water on Earth is in the oceans: about 97 percent of
it is salt water. Of the remaining three percent that is fresh, roughly
two thirds is frozen in glaciers and ice caps, mostly in Antarctica and
Greenland. Most of the rest is groundwater, held in the spaces between
grains of sand and rock below the surface. Lakes, rivers, the soil and
the air together hold well under one percent of all fresh water, yet
that small share is the water that plants, animals and people use every
day.

Water does not stay in one place. It moves between the oceans, the air,
the land and living things in a loop that has no beginning and no end.
Scientists call this loop the water cycle, or the hydrologic cycle. The
sun drives it, and gravity pulls the water back down.

## 4.2 Evaporation

Evaporation is the change of liquid water into water vapor, an
invisible gas. When sunlight warms the surface of the ocean, a lake or
a puddle, some water molecules gain enough energy to escape into the
air. Warm, dry and windy weather makes evaporation faster, because the
air can take up more vapor and the wind carries the moist air away.

The oceans supply about 86 percent of the water that evaporates around
the world. When salt water evaporates, the salt stays behind, so the
vapor that rises is fresh.

## 4.3 Transpiration

Plants also put water into the air. They take water up from the soil
through their roots, move it through their stems, and release it as
vapor through tiny pores in their leaves called stomata. This process is
called transpiration. A single large oak tree can transpire more than
150,000 liters of water in a year.

Evaporation from the soil and transpiration from plants are hard to
measure separately, so scientists often add them together and call the
total evapotranspiration.

## 4.4 Condensation

As warm, moist air rises, it cools. Cool air cannot hold as much vapor
as warm air, so some of the vapor turns back into tiny liquid droplets.
This change from gas to liquid is called condensation. The droplets
form around specks of dust, salt or smoke in the air, and billions of
them together make a cloud.

The temperature at which air becomes saturated and condensation begins
is called the dew point. Dew on the grass in the morning is
condensation too: the ground cools during the night, and the air
touching it cools below its dew point.

## 4.5 Precipitation

Inside a cloud, droplets bump into each other and join. When they grow
too heavy for the rising air to hold up, they fall as precipitation:
rain, snow, sleet or hail, depending on the temperature of the air they
fall through. A typical raindrop is made of about a million cloud
droplets.

A water molecule stays in the atmosphere for about nine days on average
before it falls back to the surface. In the ocean, by contrast, a
molecule may stay for thousands of years, and in deep groundwater or an
ice cap for much longer.

## 4.6 Runoff and infiltration

When precipitation reaches the land, some of it soaks into the ground.
This is called infiltration. How much soaks in depends on the soil: sand
lets water in quickly, while clay and paved surfaces let in very little.

Water that does not soak in flows over the surface as runoff. Runoff
gathers in streams and rivers and eventually returns to the ocean. In
cities, where roofs and roads cover much of the ground, runoff is
greater and floods come faster after heavy rain.

Water that infiltrates deep enough becomes groundwater. Layers of rock
or sediment that hold enough groundwater to supply a well are called
aquifers. Groundwater moves slowly, often only a few meters a year, and
much of it eventually seeps into rivers, lakes or the sea.

## 4.7 Why the cycle matters

The water cycle moves heat as well as water. Evaporation takes heat
from the surface, and condensation releases that heat higher in the
atmosphere, which powers storms. The cycle also cleans water: when
water evaporates, it leaves salt and most pollution behind.

People change the cycle when they pump groundwater faster than rain
can refill it, cut down forests that would transpire water into the
air, or cover land with pavement. Understanding each step of the cycle
helps communities plan how much water they can safely use.

## Key terms

- Evaporation: liquid water changing into water vapor.
- Transpiration: plants releasing water vapor through their leaves.
- Condensation: water vapor changing into liquid droplets.
- Precipitation: water falling from clouds as rain, snow, sleet or hail.
- Infiltration: water soaking into the ground.
- Aquifer: a layer of rock or sediment that holds usable groundwater.
